## 12.6 Chapter Summary

### 12.6.1 Key Takeaways

As an entirely new training paradigm, reinforcement learning enables models to learn in uncertain environments. Its central objective is to find the optimal policy, principally through two categories of methods: value-function learning and policy learning.

The mathematics of value-function learning is relatively straightforward. The basic idea is to estimate the value function—the expected score of each state—and then formulate a policy from those estimates. Because this chapter focuses on optimizing LLMs through reinforcement learning, it discussed only value-function estimation. At the algorithmic level, all the methods introduced can be classified as TD Lambda learning, with MC learning and TD learning as two special cases. Their central innovation is how they estimate the label data used for model learning. Adjusting the hyperparameter $\lambda$ balances the variance and bias of the estimate. When TD Lambda learning is combined with a neural network, model parameters are updated with the advantage function, which measures the advantage of the current action relative to the average. The advantage function bridges value-function learning and policy learning, and GAE computes it efficiently.

Policy learning is more direct but mathematically more complex. The policy gradient theorem directly updates a policy until it converges to the optimum. In an uncertain environment with incomplete data, however, learning directly from policy gradients can be unstable, so value-function estimates are used to reduce variation. More specifically, the baseline algorithm is defined with the advantage function from MC learning, while A2C is defined with the advantage function from TD learning.

When policy learning is used to optimize a large language model, the absence of a constraint on the magnitude of model changes generally leads to severe “overfitting.” PPO is introduced on top of A2C to address the problem. It first evaluates the magnitude of the model update. If the update lies within an appropriate range, training proceeds normally; otherwise, the update is stopped. Reinforcement learning is an extremely broad field, and this chapter covers only a small portion. Many mathematical details were not proved in depth. Interested readers can consult Richard S. Sutton and Andrew G. Barto's *Reinforcement Learning*.
