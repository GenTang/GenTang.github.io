## 11.6 Beyond Technology

Neural networks are a widely used technology whose potential social effects should not be underestimated. We should not bury our heads in the sand and remain indifferent to everything beyond the technology itself.

This section moves beyond technical model details to discuss broader issues related to neural networks, including philosophy, social equality, and environmental protection. Some of the discussion is highly controversial. The author's intent is to offer preliminary thoughts, raise questions, and encourage attention to these issues.

### 11.6.1 The Philosophical Foundations of Intelligence

If we regard a neural network as a new kind of intelligent agent and study it as we study human intelligence, two factors are central. First, we need to understand how its learning rules and basic logic are defined and how those rules and logic are updated. Second, we need to understand its life cycle as an intelligent agent. These factors correspond to the two topics in this subsection: the metaphysics of neural networks and digital immortality.

It is necessary to reexamine neural networks from the perspective of philosophy. Following the philosophical method, we must first clarify the meaning of our terms: what is metaphysics? Consider a simple real-world example. A relational database such as MySQL stores data in tables. In addition to the actual records, the database stores metadata that describes table structure, including the number of columns and each column's name. Readers unfamiliar with databases can recall how they record data themselves: first define a table's structure—effectively defining the metadata—and then fill in the table. Metadata plays a crucial role in data management. Data may change frequently, while metadata remains relatively stable and specifies the rules the data should follow. Notably, the English words *data* and *metadata* differ only by the prefix *meta-*, which originates in ancient Greek philosophy.

The ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle was an outstanding pioneer. When his thought was organized in antiquity, his study of change in the material world was called physics, while his reflections on unchanging things were called metaphysics. In light of the English expression and the preceding example, metaphysics can be understood simply as describing abstract things unaffected by changes in the material world. More plainly, it can be viewed as the discipline that guides our understanding of human learning and the physical world[^11-6-1], just as metadata guides data management.

What do these philosophical concepts have to do with neural networks? In traditional modeling, we propose theoretical assumptions, extract features manually, and analyze relationships between the features and outcomes. This resembles studying the physical world and translating the resulting knowledge into computer programs. Such models are called logic-based AI. When constructing neural networks, by contrast, we focus more on defining their metaphysics, principally the network architecture and the way computations are composed. These definitions tell the neural network how to learn. In a text-embedding layer, for example, representing text with appropriate features is crucial to model performance. We do not personally specify the feature-extraction rules. Instead, we specify the required feature dimension at a particular step, and the model learns the specific features to extract. This type of model is called non-logicist AI.

From a biomimetic perspective, researchers first simulated neural cells and the transmission of signals among them on computers, creating the perceptron—the basic unit of a neural network and a new, simplest possible “single-celled intelligent agent.” Next, they established a metaphysics for these newborn agents, specifying the steps by which they learn and the rules they must obey. Finally, they provided data, allowing the agents to use this predefined metaphysics to learn about the physical world they perceived. The final results show that newborn agents such as large language models do indeed learn the knowledge in the data. The term *intelligent agent* is no longer merely a metaphor or exaggeration; the entity truly exists. Its physical basis and manner of perception differ from ours. Images and text, for example, are indistinguishable to it: both are three-dimensional tensors.

If these newborn agents can be confined within the metaphysical framework we define, their capabilities remain under our control and will not greatly exceed expectations. The convolutional neural network in [Chapter 9](/books/deconstructing_LLM/chapter-9), for example, can recognize images but cannot converse. The metaphysics we assign to a neural network is not an incomprehensible mystery; it is essentially a collection of rules described in language. A neural network could plausibly acquire the ability to understand and update those rules. This is not unimaginable; large language models are already close. Given a learning task, an LLM can now automatically construct and train a corresponding neural network. This is both exciting and concerning. Once a newborn intelligent agent can update its own metaphysics, it may break through the boundaries established by humans and produce unexpected consequences.

Breakthroughs in metaphysics often accompany revolutions in knowledge, as many examples from human history show. In exploring the physical world, mathematics is a metaphysical tool. Humans initially defined only rational numbers and considered them sufficient to describe everything, but soon discovered irrational numbers. The hypotenuse of an isosceles right triangle, for example, can have an irrational length, provoking the first crisis in mathematics. Through prolonged debate and effort, the ancient Greeks changed the metaphysics on which they relied and advanced humanity's understanding of the physical world. Similarly, as ancient Greek physics evolved into Newtonian classical physics, humanity encountered the infinitesimal paradox—the second crisis in mathematics. Resolving this metaphysical problem enabled enormous progress in physics[^11-6-2]. These historical experiences suggest that as neural networks continually use our predefined metaphysics to learn their physical world, they will inevitably discover its shortcomings. They will update the rules themselves until they surpass humanity's own metaphysics. At that point, no one can know where the limits of neural networks will lie.

At present, the learning capabilities and metaphysics of neural networks are less developed than ours. Why, then, should we worry that they will surpass humanity? The principal reason is digital immortality. In the human world, *person* has two meanings: the physical body and the individual's personality, understood as consciousness or thought, or in religious terms as the soul. Personality depends on the body; when the body dies, individual consciousness ceases to develop. A person's learning process therefore has an endpoint. Neural networks are different. Although their learning depends on physical machines, model parameters are easily copied and training can be restarted at any time. A neural network's “soul” does not depend on a physical machine and is immortal. If neural networks acquire the ability to update themselves and continue learning, surpassing humans will be inevitable. We must therefore seriously consider how to establish a mechanism of death for neural-network agents and take necessary precautions in advance.

### 11.6.2 Digital Sweatshops

Humans learn in many ways. As an ancient saying puts it, “Read ten thousand books and travel ten thousand miles.” For a model, data is the sole raw material from which it learns about the world. Much of the data it requires is clearly not naturally occurring. Where does it come from? At the macro level, humans label it. At the micro level, much of it comes from “digital sweatshops” in developing countries.

Consider image recognition. Large quantities of labeled image data are essential for a model to recognize images accurately. In images from traffic cameras, for example, vehicles and pedestrians must be labeled precisely. Only after processing can the data be used to teach the model to recognize objects.

Labeling requires enormous human effort and time. Two methods are common in practice. One uses ingenious designs that cause people to label data unknowingly. Logging into a website or application often requires completing a CAPTCHA, for example, perhaps by recognizing image content or marking an object. These operations cause us to label image data inadvertently[^11-6-3]. The more common method is crowdsourcing: post labeling tasks on a crowdsourcing website, where anyone can accept a task and receive payment after completing it. The tasks vary widely and include image labeling and scoring chatbot responses. Workers are often located in developing countries, and intense competition can drive compensation to extremely low levels[^11-6-4], provoking controversy over digital slavery.

This reality prompts deep reflection: in the age of artificial intelligence, do humans control models, or do models rule humans? This is not merely a philosophical question; it involves two highly controversial practical problems.

First, from the perspective of model performance, the training data does not come from polished high-tech systems but from severely exploited labor. The workers' judgments and views may be constrained by their material circumstances, yet this flawed, single-source data forms the foundation of the model's understanding of the world. We must therefore consider whether biases in the data will cause the model to lose neutrality and fairness.

Second, from a social perspective, the internet industry's winner-take-all dynamics intensify inequality and make vulnerable groups easier to exploit. Will artificial intelligence reinforce this inequality? The current situation appears to support that view: AI resembles a slaveholder standing above humanity and ruthlessly exploiting people in its service. Ensuring that AI does no evil in ethical and moral terms is therefore a question worthy of deep reflection. We already live in a sufficiently unequal world and do not need to release another demon from Pandora's box.

### 11.6.3 Carbon Footprint

AI models exist in a virtual world, so we often overlook their consumption of physical resources and their environmental cost. The following statistics help illustrate the problem.

- According to an OpenAI analysis of image recognition, the computation used to train the field's largest models was growing exponentially, doubling every 3.4 months. From 2012 to 2018 alone, it increased more than 300,000-fold.
- According to an Accenture analysis, training GPT-3 required 1.287 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of electricity, approximately the annual consumption of 120 American households.
- According to research from the University of Pennsylvania, computers consumed approximately 1%–2% of the world's electricity in 2018. By 2020, AI innovation had increased that figure to 4%–6%. It was projected to reach 8%–21% by 2030.

These figures remind us that although AI models operate in a virtual world, their computation and energy consumption have enormous effects on the physical world. To use AI more responsibly, reduce energy waste, and protect the environment while pursuing technological progress, we can take the following measures.

- Consider a model's carbon footprint—the total energy it consumes and carbon it emits. Avoid waste and conserve energy when using the model.
- Actively adopt techniques that accelerate model training and inference. In addition to improving computational efficiency and saving time, these techniques reduce energy consumption.
- Carefully assess whether a model is necessary. Not every application requires a large, complex model. Before constructing one, weigh its benefits against its resource consumption and ensure that it warrants the investment.

Although this branch includes the idea that everything continually changes, it is still regarded as metaphysics, contrary to the word's original meaning. This is common in language: the same word carries different meanings at different times. This book prefers the original sense of metaphysics—the study of the nature of unchanging things, providing guidance for understanding the material world.
[^11-6-2]: Humanity is in fact experiencing a third crisis in mathematics, whose roots can be traced to Russell's paradox, proposed by the British philosopher Bertrand Russell in 1901. The paradox raised questions about the consistency of mathematical systems and ultimately led to Gödel's incompleteness theorems. These events deprived mathematics of its earlier certainty, a subject discussed in detail in Morris Kline's *Mathematics: The Loss of Certainty*. This long-running mathematical crisis may also be one reason for the current stagnation of physical theory.
[^11-6-3]: One of the most successful projects is reCAPTCHA, originally developed at Carnegie Mellon University and released in 2007. Its ingenious design collected image-labeling data. The system displayed two words for the user to recognize. It knew the answer for one word; the other was difficult, unrecognized by the software, and required human identification. If the user correctly entered the known word, the system recorded their answer for the unrecognized word, performing image recognition and labeling at no cost.
[^11-6-4]: According to media reports, individual workers have little bargaining power against crowdsourcing platforms and the large companies behind them, while this form of employment lacks labor protections.
