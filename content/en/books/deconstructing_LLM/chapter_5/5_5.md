## 5.5 Chapter Summary

### 5.5.1 Key Takeaways

Rather than introducing a specific model, this chapter draws on ideas from econometrics to discuss two common types of problems in modeling practice.

The first is feature processing. The features used by a model fall into two categories: quantitative and qualitative. Quantitative features support numerical operations directly and can therefore be used in a model as they are. This use, however, implies an assumption of a constant marginal effect. To eliminate that hidden assumption, a quantitative feature can be converted into a qualitative feature through binning.

Qualitative features do not support numerical operations directly and therefore usually must be converted into multiple dummy variables. These dummy variables carry relatively little information and offer limited help in improving model performance, so it is also worth attempting to convert qualitative features into quantitative ones. Traditional methods generally target ordinal qualitative features, and many specific algorithms are available. This chapter focused on Ridit scoring, which is widely used in the insurance industry.

The second problem is the effect of adding features to a model. Usually, increasing the number of features also increases the amount of information available to the model and improves its predictive performance. In some cases, however, more features are not a “blessing” but a “curse.” Too many features can readily produce multicollinearity, destabilizing the entire model and impairing both its predictive and explanatory capabilities.

Beyond the topics discussed in this chapter, econometrics has another, even more important subject: model endogeneity. Endogeneity means that labels and features influence one another. It can seriously affect model-parameter estimation and thereby reduce model accuracy. The issue resembles the philosophical question of whether the chicken or the egg came first. It has no clear engineering solution, and applications in artificial intelligence are exceedingly rare. Despite its importance, endogeneity is therefore not covered in this book.

Readers interested in the topic should consult other literature, such as Jeffrey Wooldridge's *Introductory Econometrics: A Modern Approach* and Russell Davidson et al.'s *Econometric Theory and Methods*.

Through this chapter, the book has focused on traditional statistical models, also known as data models. These models are generally stable and easy to interpret, but their predictive performance is often unsatisfactory when they are used alone in real applications. To compensate for this limitation, the following chapters focus on another category: algorithmic models. This field encompasses many models, of which neural networks are a classic example and a core component of artificial intelligence. Such models generally achieve excellent predictive performance, but their stability and interpretability are comparatively weaker.
