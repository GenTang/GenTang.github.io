# Chapter 1: Introduction

> Un auteur ne nuit jamais tant à ses lecteurs que quand il dissimule une difficulté.
>
> (An author never harms his readers so much as when he conceals a difficulty.)
>
> — Évariste Galois

Philosophy has an important habit worth adopting: before discussing any question, one must carefully examine the precise definitions of the terms being used, because they form the foundation of the discussion. This book follows the same practice by first defining what it means by artificial intelligence. Artificial intelligence can be regarded as a new kind of perceptive intelligent agent, with data as its material foundation and models as the basis of its intelligence. This description is neither alarmist nor needlessly profound. The field of artificial intelligence is extraordinarily broad, and we need to look beyond technical details and examine it from a high-level perspective in order to see both the trees and the forest. Moreover, artificial intelligence has already passed through its infancy and reached a considerable level of maturity; it may even have developed some degree of self-awareness. Human beings must therefore set aside their sense of superiority over artificial intelligence and seek to understand this emerging intelligent agent as an equal.

To understand artificial intelligence, we should first examine how human beings perceive the world. The five human senses—sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch—correspond respectively to the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, and body. Through them, we perceive various stimuli from the physical world and convert those stimuli into nerve impulses, or electrical signals, which are then sent to the brain for processing. The brain processes these signals in some fashion and sends signals back to the sensory organs, again in the form of nerve impulses, prompting the organs to react accordingly. Consider the Chinese idiom “quenching thirst by thinking of plums.” When the eyes see plums, they convert the image into nerve impulses and transmit them to the brain. The brain then responds by sending information to the tongue, triggering the secretion of saliva. The entire chain through which human beings perceive the world can be divided into two independent parts: the interaction between nerve impulses and the physical world, and the interaction between nerve impulses and the brain. This distinction is vividly illustrated in the classic science-fiction film *The Matrix*: machines trap human beings in an illusory simulated world by reproducing various nerve impulses, while the humans remain unaware of the deception. Although their bodies are suspended in pods, they believe that they are still living in the real physical world. Human intelligence, then, effectively resides in the interaction between nerve impulses and the brain. In this interaction, nerve impulses constitute the material foundation, while the brain provides the basis for intelligence. Something almost identical occurs in artificial intelligence: data correspond to the physical world and are, in essence, a mirror of it. Data therefore become the material foundation of artificial intelligence, and it naturally follows that the models responsible for processing these data provide the basis for its intelligence.

Both foundations described above—the material foundation and the basis of intelligence—belong to the empirical world. In Plato’s philosophical system, however, there is also a world of Forms beyond the empirical world, and this is where human self-awareness resides.[^1] We are therefore led to ask: might artificial intelligence, with analogous foundations, likewise possess some degree of self-awareness?

[^1]: Plato was a renowned ancient Greek philosopher. His theory of the empirical world and the world of Forms is, in fact, closely connected to everyday life. Consider a simple example: we live on Earth and perceive curved surfaces around us—the empirical world—yet geometry concerns itself entirely with planes in the world of Forms.
