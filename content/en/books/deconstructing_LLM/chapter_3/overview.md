The linear regression model is so intuitive and concise that its importance is often overlooked. In fact, linear regression is a cornerstone of artificial intelligence and provides strong support for the construction of many complex models. The following joke about a mathematician offers a vivid analogy for the importance of linear regression in artificial intelligence.

One day, a mathematician grew tired of mathematics and suddenly went to a fire station to become a firefighter. The fire chief said, “You look promising, but first you must pass a test.” They walked to an alley behind the station, where there was a warehouse, a fire hydrant, and a hose. The chief asked, “If the warehouse were on fire, what would you do?” The mathematician replied, “I would connect the hose to the hydrant, turn on the water, and put out the fire.” The chief nodded. “Exactly right! But suppose you walked into the alley and the warehouse was not on fire. What would you do?” The mathematician thought for a moment and answered, “I would set the warehouse on fire.” The chief was shocked. “Why? That would be extremely dangerous! Why would you set the warehouse on fire?” The mathematician replied, “Because then I would have reduced the problem to one I already know how to solve.”

Using models to solve practical problems follows a similar line of thought. When confronted with an unfamiliar problem, we always try to transform it mathematically into one that an existing model can solve. Even in today's most complex deep neural networks, such as large language models, careful examination reveals a dense collection of what are, for the most part, linear regression models. From a physiological perspective, moreover, the human brain has difficulty processing nonlinear relationships directly and tends to simplify complex relationships into linear ones. This suggests that linear models align with both our intuitive choices and our innate capabilities. No matter how complex or sophisticated a model may be, linear models are indispensable within it. Mastering linear models is therefore fundamental to understanding and working with complex models.

Even when used on its own, a linear regression model can play an important role in many situations. Linear regression, for example, is widely used in economics. In fact, many economic policies that affect us are products of linear regression models[^is-lm]. Building a model does not mean perfectly simulating the real world; it means constructing a process of continual approximation. This is why data scientists often say that “all models are wrong.” But the statement has an important qualification: “some are useful.” A “useful” model filters out unimportant details in the data, captures the principal underlying relationships, and helps us understand and interpret the data more effectively. In many situations, a linear model is precisely such a “useful” model: concise, efficient, and easy to understand. If a linear model is already sufficiently “useful,” why expend effort building a complex model that is difficult to understand and may introduce new problems?[^occam]

Linear models are not only the starting point of artificial intelligence but also important tools for solving real-world problems. Their clear mathematical foundations and broad range of applications make them an excellent entry point for learning and mastering artificial intelligence.

[^is-lm]: The IS–LM model in Keynesian economics consists of two linear models. The IS model represents the investment–saving relationship and focuses on the connection between national saving and market interest rates. The LM model represents the liquidity preference–money supply relationship and studies the relationship between national income and the demand for money. In concise form, these linear models describe complex economic phenomena and provide powerful tools for explaining macroeconomics.
[^occam]: Artificial intelligence follows an important philosophical principle known as Occam's razor, or the law of parsimony. It is widely applied to model construction and theory selection, and its basic meaning can be summarized as “entities should not be multiplied without necessity.” In other words, if multiple theories can accurately predict the same phenomenon, the theory with the fewest assumptions should be chosen. Although more complex methods often produce more accurate predictions, if the difference in predictive performance is not significant, the method with the simplest assumptions should be selected. This principle is commonly followed when building models. The classic example of Occam's razor comes from astronomy. When heliocentrism first emerged, both geocentrism and heliocentrism could explain the motion of the Sun, the Moon, and the other planets. At the time, both theories offered reasonable explanations. Heliocentrism, however, required only seven basic assumptions, whereas geocentrism required many additional ones. According to Occam's razor, heliocentrism should therefore be chosen as the simpler and more effective explanation.
